# Budgeting

## What is a budget?
A budget is a plan for how you will spend and save your money over a period of time, usually one month.

Budgeting helps you:
- Understand where your money goes
- Avoid overspending
- Save for future goals
- Prepare for emergencies

## Common spending categories
- Housing or rent
- Food and groceries
- Transportation
- Utilities
- Education
- Entertainment
- Healthcare/insurance
- Savings

## Budgeting tips
- Track your spending.
- Separate needs from wants.
- Save before spending when possible.
- Review your budget every month.

---

# Saving Money

Saving means setting aside money for future needs or goals.

Examples:
- Emergency fund
- Tuition fees
- Laptop
- Travel
- New phone

Good saving habits:
- Save consistently.
- Set realistic goals.
- Avoid unnecessary impulse purchases.

---

# Bank Accounts

Savings Account
- Used for everyday savings.
- Usually earns interest.
- Suitable for most students.

Current Account
- Designed mainly for businesses.
- Usually has higher transaction limits.

Fixed Deposit (FD)
- Money is locked for a fixed period.
- Usually offers higher interest than a savings account.

Recurring Deposit (RD)
- Deposit a fixed amount regularly.
- Useful for disciplined saving.

---

# Digital Payments

Digital payments allow money to be transferred electronically.

Examples include:
- Bank transfers
- Debit cards
- Credit cards
- Mobile wallets
- Country-specific payment systems (for example, UPI in India)

Never share OTPs, PINs, or passwords.

---

# Credit Cards

A credit card lets you borrow money from a bank up to a credit limit.

Best practices:
- Pay the full bill on time.
- Avoid spending beyond your ability to repay.
- Don't treat the credit limit as extra income.

---

# Credit Scores

A credit score estimates how reliably someone repays borrowed money.

Factors that affect it:
- Paying bills on time
- Credit utilization
- Length of credit history
- Number of loan applications

Higher scores generally improve chances of loan approval.

---

# Loans

Common types:
- Student loans
- Personal loans
- Home loans
- Vehicle loans

Before taking a loan:
- Understand the interest rate.
- Know the repayment period.
- Make sure repayments fit your budget.

---

# Interest

Simple Interest:
Interest is calculated only on the original amount.

Compound Interest:
Interest is calculated on both the original amount and previously earned interest.

Compound interest helps savings grow faster over long periods.

---

# Emergency Fund

An emergency fund is money saved for unexpected expenses.

Examples:
- Medical emergencies
- Job loss
- Urgent travel
- Major repairs

Aim to build it gradually over time.

---

# Investing

Investing means putting money into assets that may grow in value over time.

Common investments:
- Mutual funds
- Stocks
- Bonds
- Fixed deposits

General principles:
- Higher potential returns usually involve higher risk.
- Diversification helps reduce risk.
- Invest according to your goals and risk tolerance.

# FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid)

## What is FAFSA?
The Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) is the official application used by the U.S. government to determine a student's eligibility for federal financial aid for college.

---

## Who should apply?
Students planning to attend eligible colleges or universities in the United States should complete the FAFSA if they wish to be considered for federal financial aid.

Many colleges and states also use FAFSA information to award their own financial aid.

---

## Types of Aid Available
Students who complete the FAFSA may be considered for:

- Federal Pell Grants
- Federal Work-Study
- Federal Direct Subsidized Loans
- Federal Direct Unsubsidized Loans
- Some state financial aid programs
- Institutional grants and scholarships offered by colleges

---

## Eligibility (General)
Eligibility requirements may include:

- U.S. citizen or eligible noncitizen
- Valid Social Security Number (except certain exceptions)
- High school diploma or equivalent
- Enrolled or accepted into an eligible college program
- Maintaining satisfactory academic progress

---

## Information Needed to Complete FAFSA
Students may need:

- Social Security Number
- FSA ID
- Tax information
- Income information
- Parent income information (if considered a dependent student)
- Bank account balances
- Records of investments (if applicable)

---

## Dependency Status
FAFSA determines whether a student is:

- Dependent
- Independent

This affects whether parental financial information must be included.

Dependency status is based on FAFSA rules, not whether the student lives independently.

---

## FAFSA Deadline
FAFSA opens once each application cycle.

Students should apply as early as possible because some financial aid is awarded on a first-come, first-served basis.

Students should also check:

- Federal deadline
- State deadline
- College-specific deadlines

---

## Cost
Submitting the FAFSA is completely free.

Students should never pay a company to submit the FAFSA.

---

## Common Mistakes
Avoid:

- Missing deadlines
- Entering incorrect Social Security Numbers
- Using estimated income when finalized tax information is available
- Forgetting to sign the application
- Listing the wrong colleges

---

## FAFSA Renewal
Students generally need to submit a FAFSA for each academic year they wish to receive federal financial aid.

---

## FAFSA vs CSS Profile

FAFSA:
- Required for federal student aid
- Free to submit
- Used by most colleges

CSS Profile:
- Separate application
- Required by some colleges
- Used mainly for institutional financial aid
- May require an application fee

---

## FAFSA for International Students
Most international students are not eligible for U.S. federal financial aid through FAFSA.

Students who are not U.S. citizens or eligible noncitizens should instead:

- Check scholarships offered directly by universities.
- Look for merit scholarships.
- Explore private scholarships available to international students.
- Review financial aid policies of each university.

---

## Personalized Questions Finly Should Ask

Before giving FAFSA guidance, ask:

1. Are you planning to study in the United States?
2. Are you a U.S. citizen or eligible noncitizen?
3. Are you applying for undergraduate or graduate studies?
4. Have you already completed the FAFSA?
5. Which university or universities are you applying to?

---

## Response Tips for Finly

If the student is not studying in the U.S.:
Explain that FAFSA is only for eligible students attending U.S. institutions and recommend exploring scholarships or financial aid available in their own country or from the university.

If the student is an international applicant:
Clarify that FAFSA is generally unavailable and suggest university-specific scholarships and external scholarships.

If the student is eligible:
Explain what FAFSA is, why it is important, and encourage submitting it early before deadlines.

# Ivy League Universities and Financial Aid (2026)

The Ivy League consists of eight private universities in the United States:

- Harvard University
- Yale University
- Princeton University
- Columbia University
- Brown University
- Dartmouth College
- University of Pennsylvania (UPenn)
- Cornell University

## Important Facts

- The annual cost of attendance (tuition, housing, meals, books, and other expenses) is typically between **USD 90,000 and USD 100,000 per year**.
- All Ivy League universities meet **100% of demonstrated financial need** for admitted students.
- Most financial aid is awarded as **need-based grants**, which do not need to be repaid.
- Most Ivy League universities **do not offer merit scholarships** based on grades, test scores, sports, or extracurricular achievements.
- Financial aid is calculated individually for every admitted student.

# Regional Bank Account & Savings Guide

## United States (US)
- Savings Accounts: High-Yield Savings Accounts (HYSA) are best for emergency funds and earning higher interest than standard brick-and-mortar bank accounts.
- Retirement & Investment: Roth IRA (contributions made with after-tax money, tax-free growth) and Traditional 401(k) / IRA.
- Credit Score System: FICO score (ranges 300 to 850). Main factors: payment history (35%) and credit utilization (30%).

## United Kingdom (UK)
- Savings Accounts: Individual Savings Accounts (ISA) allow UK residents to save or invest tax-free up to £20,000 per tax year. Cash ISAs are ideal for cash savings; Lifetime ISAs (LISA) offer a 25% government bonus for first-time home buyers or retirement.
- Current Accounts: Look for fee-free student current accounts that offer interest-free overdrafts (e.g., Santander, HSBC, NatWest).
- Credit System: Monitored by agencies like Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion.

## India
- Savings Accounts: High-interest savings accounts or Digital Savings Accounts.
- Fixed Deposits (FD) & Recurring Deposits (RD): FDs offer higher guaranteed interest for locked periods. RDs allow fixed monthly savings.
- Investments & Tax Saving: Public Provident Fund (PPF), Equity Linked Savings Scheme (ELSS), and Systematic Investment Plans (SIP) in mutual funds.
- Payments: Unified Payments Interface (UPI) for instant transfers.

## Canada
- Savings Accounts: Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) allows tax-free growth on investments and savings. First Home Savings Account (FHSA) for first-time homebuyers.
- Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP): Tax-deductible contributions for long-term retirement savings.

# Scenario-Based Budgeting Frameworks

## Scenario A: Student or Low-Income (Income < $1,500 / £1,200 monthly)
- Strategy: Envelope System or Zero-Based Budgeting.
- Recommendation: Standard rules like 50/30/20 may be unrealistic because fixed costs (rent, food) absorb most income. Focus on tracking every expense and building a small starter emergency fund ($500 / £500).

## Scenario B: Stable Income / New Graduate
- Strategy: 50/30/20 Rule.
  - 50% Needs: Rent, groceries, utilities, minimum debt payments.
  - 30% Wants: Dining out, subscriptions, leisure, travel.
  - 20% Savings/Debt: Emergency fund growth, investments, debt payoff above minimums.

## Scenario C: Irregular or Freelance Income
- Strategy: Baseline Budgeting.
- Recommendation: Calculate average income from the lowest-earning month of the past year. Base fixed monthly living expenses on that minimum baseline. Keep a larger emergency fund (6–9 months of expenses).

# Decision Tree: Debt vs. Saving Priorities

When a user asks whether to pay off debt or save money first, use the following rules:

1. High-Interest Debt (> 8% APR, such as credit cards or payday loans):
   - Priority: Build a small starter emergency fund ($500–$1,000) first to avoid taking on new debt during unexpected events.
   - Action: Aggressively pay down high-interest debt using the Avalanche Method (highest interest rate first) or Snowball Method (smallest balance first).

2. Low-Interest Debt (< 4% APR, such as subsidized student loans or low-rate auto loans):
   - Priority: Make minimum required payments on time.
   - Action: Direct extra money toward building a full 3–6 month emergency fund or investing in long-term savings accounts.

---

# Decision Tree: Emergency Fund Targets

Guide users on emergency fund sizing based on their life context:

- Living at home / Full student support: $500 – $1,000 buffer (covers minor repair, emergency travel, or textbook costs).
- Renting student / Single adult with stable job: 3 to 6 months of essential living costs (rent, food, basic utilities).
- Freelancer / Sole household earner / Variable income: 6 to 9 months of full living costs.
